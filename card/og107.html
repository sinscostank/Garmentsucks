OG-107 is a colour, not a cloth — and it had a low-IR dye

OG-107 is a colour code that people keep mistaking for a cloth — and the specification hides a detail almost no reproduction copies.

established

Two garments share the name. The utility uniform in Olive Green 107 was cotton sateen: dense, wind-resistant, and a poor choice in humid heat. The tropical combat jacket in the same shade was poplin, and later ripstop — light, open, quick to dry. Same colour, opposite behaviour. Most of the confusion in the reproduction market begins exactly here.

The colour itself is a code, not a fabric. Documents trace the American olive family from the lighter Olive Drab No. 3 of the early war years to the darker No. 7 adopted in 1943 for concealment in European vegetation, and then to the standardised shade that clothed every service from the 1950s. The exact standardisation date is the kind of detail that should be checked against the original colour standard rather than repeated.

The more interesting fact is buried in a fabric specification. MIL-C-342 requires the tropical cloth to carry a low infra-red dye formulation. In plain terms: a dye chosen so the cloth behaves correctly in the near-infrared, the band where foliage is bright and sensors read the world differently. That is a design decision made at the level of chemistry, and it is the sort of thing a reproduction can only imitate by accident.

What this means for a buyer is simple. When a jacket is described as OG-107, ask which OG-107: the sateen utility or the tropical poplin. One is a sponge in the monsoon and the other is the reason the tropical uniform worked. The shade is the same; the garment is not.

What to look for

Identify the cloth before the colour. Sateen has a smooth, lustrous face and holds water; poplin is crisp and dries; ripstop shows a woven grid.
